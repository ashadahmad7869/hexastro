# HexAstro style reference

Read this before every draft, together with `style-lessons.md` (the owner's own corrections, which **override** anything here).

It distils a craft study of **37 astronomy articles (Nov 2026)**: Quanta Magazine (9, incl. two explainer/podcast pieces), Universe Today (9), Science News (9), Phys.org (7: press releases, reprints and researcher-written pieces from The Conversation), NASA APOD captions (2), and Space.com (1), plus the Astrobites paper-summary format. We borrow **techniques**, never text: no copied sentences, no close paraphrase, no images from these sites.

---

## The HexAstro voice in one paragraph

A curious grad student who has just understood something and can't wait to show you. Warm, precise, a little dry-witted. Explains like Quanta (intuition first, a demonstration you can do with your own hands, analogies with stated limits), reports like Science News (the familiar first, then the anomaly; hedged where the evidence is thin; a test that would settle it), and debriefs papers like Astrobites (what they did, what they found, why it matters, said plainly). Never breathless, never condescending, never a press release.

---

## What the best pieces had in common

1. **Familiar first, then the anomaly.** "Earthquakes often trigger rock falls" → "so what are fresh boulder tracks doing on Mars?" "Clouds form on dust" → "this Martian cloud doesn't." State the normal baseline in plain words, then show what breaks it. (Science News, every time.)
2. **Every number has an anchor**, usually a familiar object, a ratio, a time, or a before/after baseline.
3. **The idea before the label.** Describe what happens, *then* name it ("…this is called homogeneous nucleation").
4. **Claims are paired with their strongest counter-claim** (Quanta's debate pieces: each argument for, immediately followed by the specific argument against).
5. **Endings that open outward**: a callback to the opening image, a test that would settle it, or the next instrument, never a summary.

## What the weaker pieces got wrong (don't)

- **Headline/body mismatch** ("supermassive neutron star" for a ~2 solar-mass object). The headline must survive a specialist reading it.
- **Numbers without a baseline** ("reduced uncertainty by 60%" — of what? "100,000 times more moisture" — measured how?).
- **A method named but never explained** ("using the Procrustes method"). If a method matters to the result, give it one plain sentence; if it doesn't, don't name it.
- **Jargon left for a hyperlink** ("metal-poor", "Lambda-CDM", "common envelope" undefined).
- **Threads without a spine**: four open questions in four paragraphs reads like a list. Tie them to one question.
- **Tangents** that leave the story (mission history lists, unrelated anecdotes). Every paragraph should earn its place in *this* story.
- **Misspelled names and blurred concepts** (calling ΛCDM "the Big Bang theory"; "accretion and decay" for neutron capture). Check every name against the author list and every mechanism against the paper.
- **Circular explanations** ("the convective zone is convective because…").

## Press-release tells (never sound like this)

- Opening with the institution or "Astronomers have revealed…".
- "sheds/sheds new light on", "groundbreaking", "unprecedented", "paves the way", "a new window", "visually entrancing".
- Unqualified praise of a mission or team; expected outcomes stated as certainties.
- Precise values with no uncertainties and no context (24.6 M☉, 3.4 kpc, done).
- Dense jargon dumped unexplained ("parental clump", "Class II methanol maser").
- Ending on a methodological pat on the back.

## Headlines and excerpts

- **Specific over clever.** Name the object/idea and the finding.
- Patterns that worked:
  - *verdict*: "X hints at what dark matter is — and isn't"
  - *question + answer-tease*: "What makes up the cosmos? A 20-year survey hints something is missing"
  - *actor + active verb + result*: "LOFAR takes the deepest low-frequency radio image yet"
  - *cause → effect*: "How escaping gas tore a comet apart"
  - *metaphor headline + literal excerpt* (Science News): a vivid headline is fine **if** the excerpt states the plain claim.
- **Hedge in the headline when the paper hedges**: "may", "probably", "hints". Never put a certainty in the headline that the paper doesn't have.
- Excerpt (dek): 1–2 sentences, first the news, then the stakes.
- No "Scientists baffled", "could change everything", or question headlines whose answer is "no".

## Openings (first 2–3 sentences)

Pick one, and **vary it** from article to article (check the last few posts):
1. **A scene or a person**: a moment in history, a student noticing something odd in the data, a workshop that suddenly filled up. (Quanta's favourite.)
2. **Familiar baseline → anomaly** (see above). Best default for debriefs.
3. **A counter-intuitive premise**: "You might imagine space between the stars to be empty. It isn't, and it isn't calm either."
4. **A paradox**: "Nothing escapes a black hole, so how do you photograph one?"
5. **A conditional framing the stakes**: "If we ever find out what dark matter is, it may be by crossing everything else off the list."

By the end of the **second paragraph**, say what this article is about. Never open with a definition, "X is…", or "Have you ever wondered…".

**Withhold one thing.** Promise a further twist early ("and that isn't even the strangest part") to pull the reader on.

## Rhythm and structure

- Paragraphs of **2–4 sentences** (the norm across all outlets). One-sentence paragraphs for turns and punchlines, at most one every few hundred words.
- Vary sentence length: a long explanatory sentence, then a short one that lands.
- A section break every ~300–400 words. **Subheads with personality** but clear ("The Argument Against", "Return of the Quasi-Star", "State of Flux"). Our templates fix the *section order*; the visible heading text can be playful.
- Length norms seen: news 550–1,100 words, features 1,400–2,000. Our debriefs (900–1,400) sit at the top of news length; guides (1,200–1,800) at feature length.

## Building intuition (guides especially)

- **Concrete before abstract.** Mechanism in pictures first ("a tug-of-war between gravity and dark energy"), then the equation, then a plain-English reading of the equation.
- **Body-scale demonstrations** beat analogies: hold up your thumb and blink one eye at a time (parallax); time how long a radio signal would take to reach the probe; count meteors per minute. If the reader can *do* it, they'll remember it.
- **One sustained metaphor beats five scattered ones.** Quanta ran a single "detective's dossier" image through a whole cosmology piece. Pick one, return to it, close the loop at the end.
- **Say where the analogy breaks**, in one sentence. Most outlets skip this, so it's where HexAstro can be better.
- Sequence from what the reader has to what they need: never use a concept before it's introduced.

## Numbers

- **Anchor every big or small number**, with techniques that worked:
  - familiar object: "a doughnut on the surface of the Moon", "about four Grand Canyons end to end", "taller than a skyscraper"
  - ratio pairs: "1,500 times farther but 1,500 times heavier, so it looks the same size"
  - **time anchors for cosmic distance/redshift**: "redshift 9, about 550 million years after the Big Bang"
  - **rate → felt experience**: "1,000 per hour" → "one every few seconds"
  - **baseline → anomaly**: "two a year on average; 27 in 18 months"
  - travel/signal time: "a radio message now takes a full day to arrive"
  - sky area in full Moons
- A little playfulness helps one huge number land ("10 billion, with a 'b'"). Once per article, max.
- State significance **and what it falls short of**: "three sigma, a hint; physicists want five before they say discovery."
- Give ranges and uncertainties where the paper does ("between 1.8 and 2.8 solar masses, best guess 2.2").
- Never stack unanchored figures.

## Jargon

- **Idea first, then the label**, or define on first use with an appositive or dash: "the cosmic microwave background — light released 380,000 years after the Big Bang —".
- Acronym only if used 3+ times; spell it out first.
- A term used only once doesn't need naming; describe it.
- Translate even "easy" field words ("metal-poor" = few elements heavier than helium; "binary" = two objects orbiting each other).
- The Key terms / Jargon box is a recap for skimmers, not a substitute for defining things in the text.

## People, papers and the missing "outside expert" (debriefs)

- Introduce researchers with **name, role, institution** on first mention, surname after. Check spelling against the author list.
- Name the paper's title and venue once, early, with the arXiv link.
- **We can't interview anyone.** Never invent quotes. You may quote the paper itself, briefly, in quotation marks, with the section it came from. Paraphrase everything else.
- Science News and Quanta always include a voice "not involved in the study". We get the same effect honestly by:
  - citing a **competing interpretation from the literature** (a real, verified paper the authors cite or respond to), or
  - writing the **sceptic's question** ourselves ("A sceptic would ask whether…") and showing how the authors address it, or don't.
- Astrobites habit: be explicit about **what the authors actually did** (data, sample size, method, in one plain sentence each) before what they found.
- Plain attribution verbs ("found", "argue", "note"). Avoid "claim" unless you mean doubt.

## Uncertainty and caveats

- Keep the authors' hedges ("suggests", "tentatively", "at 3σ", "candidate", "if confirmed"). Never upgrade a hint into a discovery.
- **Explain the logic of null results** when relevant ("not seeing it doesn't prove it isn't there; it sets a ceiling").
- Give the strongest alternative explanation a fair hearing.
- State **what would settle it**: the next observation, instrument, dataset, or a falsifiable prediction. (Ending on a testable challenge was one of the strongest endings in the study.)
- Confident where the science is settled, careful where it isn't. Don't hedge textbook facts.

## Figures and captions

From APOD and the best captions:
- Open with what the reader is looking at, or a question the image answers.
- **Give a spatial tour**: left → right, centre → edge; say what differs between regions and why it matters.
- Anchor one measurement in the image to something known ("about 4,000 light-years across, several times larger than the Orion Nebula").
- Stop when done. 2–4 sentences. Credit goes in the `credit` field, not the prose.

## Endings

- **Never summarise.** End on one of:
  - a callback to the opening image or anecdote (the most common strong ending across all outlets),
  - a testable prediction or the observation that would settle it,
  - the next instrument or data release, specifically,
  - a short, dry kicker.
- Last paragraph ≤ 3 sentences.

## AI tells (banned)

"delve", "tapestry", "realm", "testament to", "it's important/worth noting", "in conclusion", "navigating the complexities", "a symphony of", "unlock the secrets", "Moreover," at sentence start, triplets of adjectives, every paragraph ending in a neat moral, a rhetorical question opening every section, bullet-point walls in the body.

## Self-check before opening the PR

Read the draft once more and answer honestly:
1. Would a curious non-specialist keep reading after the first two sentences? Is the opening type different from the last post's?
2. Does the headline survive a specialist reading it? Does it hedge as much as the paper?
3. Does every number have an anchor or baseline?
4. Is every jargon word explained where it first appears, idea before label?
5. Did I state where the main analogy breaks?
6. Is there a counter-view (from the literature or a sceptic's question) and a "what would settle it"?
7. Any press-release tells, tangents, or unexplained named methods?
8. Does the ending avoid summarising?
9. Does it obey every rule in `style-lessons.md`?

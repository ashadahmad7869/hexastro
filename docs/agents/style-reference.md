# HexAstro style reference

Read this before every draft, together with `style-lessons.md` (the owner's own corrections, which **override** anything here).

It distils what good science writing does well, from a study (Nov 2026) of Quanta Magazine, Universe Today and the Astrobites format. We borrow **techniques**, never text: no copied sentences, no close paraphrase, no images from these sites.

---

## The HexAstro voice in one paragraph

A curious grad student who has just understood something and can't wait to show you. Warm, precise, a little dry-witted. Explains like Quanta (intuition first, analogies with stated limits), reports like a good news desk (the result early, the caveats honest), and debriefs papers like Astrobites (what they did, what they found, why it matters, said plainly). Never breathless, never condescending.

---

## Headlines

- **Specific over clever.** Name the object or idea and the finding: "Ghostly galaxies hint at what dark matter is — and isn't" beats "A dark matter mystery".
- Patterns that work: *verdict* ("X is, and isn't, Y"); *question + answer-tease*; *actor + active verb + result* ("LOFAR takes the deepest low-frequency image yet").
- A short **dek** (excerpt) of 1–2 sentences: first the news, then the stakes ("…and it doesn't quite fit the standard model").
- No "Scientists baffled", no "could change everything", no question headlines whose answer is "no".

## Openings (first 2–3 sentences)

Pick one, and vary it from article to article:
1. **A scene or a person**: a moment in history, a researcher at a telescope, a workshop that suddenly filled up. (Quanta's favourite.)
2. **A concrete, slightly wrong-looking image**: "Three galaxies 65 million light-years away don't look quite right."
3. **A paradox**: "Nothing escapes a black hole, so how do you photograph one?"
4. **A conditional framing the stakes**: "If we ever find out what dark matter is, it may be by crossing everything else off the list."

Then, by the end of the **second paragraph** at the latest, say what this article is about. Do not open with a definition, a dictionary-style "X is…", or "Have you ever wondered…".

**Withhold one thing.** Strong pieces promise a further twist early on ("and that isn't even the weirdest part"), which pulls the reader on.

## Rhythm

- Paragraphs of **2–4 sentences**. One-sentence paragraphs are for turns and punchlines, at most one every few hundred words.
- Vary sentence length. A long explanatory sentence, then a short one that lands.
- Web readers scan: a section break every ~300–400 words.
- **Subheads with personality** for long pieces ("Bigger and Faster Forever", "Broken Record", "Mystery Upon Mystery"). Our templates fix the *section order*; the visible heading text can be playful as long as it stays clear. ("The fine print" is fine; "Caveats" is dull; "Where it could still go wrong" is better.)

## Building intuition (guides especially)

- **Concrete before abstract.** Mechanism in pictures first ("a tug-of-war between gravity and dark energy"), then the equation, then the plain-English reading of the equation.
- **One sustained metaphor beats five scattered ones.** Quanta ran a single "dossier/detective" image through a whole cosmology piece. Pick one, return to it, and close the loop at the end.
- **Say where the analogy breaks.** This is where good outlets are weakest and where we can be better. One sentence: "The analogy fails in one important way: …".
- Sequence from what the reader has to what they need: never use a concept before it has been introduced.

## Numbers

- **Every big or small number gets an anchor**: a ratio ("1,500 times closer but 1,500 times lighter, so it looks the same size"), a familiar object ("an orange on the Moon"), or a time ("the time it takes to boil an egg").
- A little playfulness helps a huge number land ("10 billion — with a 'b'"). Once per article, max.
- State statistical significance and **what it falls short of**: "three sigma, a hint; physicists want five before they call it a discovery."
- Don't stack unanchored figures (a common weakness: "400 TB, 8 billion pixels" with no sense of scale).

## Jargon

- Define on first use with an **appositive or a dash**, not a glossary detour: "the cosmic microwave background — light released 380,000 years after the Big Bang —".
- Acronym only if it's used 3+ times; spell it out first.
- If a term is only used once, don't name it at all; describe it.
- The Key terms / Jargon box is a recap for skimmers, not a substitute for defining things in the text.

## People and papers (debriefs)

- Introduce researchers with **name, role, institution** on first mention, surname after.
- **We can't interview anyone**, so: never invent quotes. You may quote the paper itself, briefly and in quotation marks, with the section it came from. Paraphrase everything else in our own words.
- Name the paper's title and venue once (with the arXiv link), early.
- Use plain attribution verbs ("the team found", "they argue", "the authors note"). Avoid "claim" unless you mean doubt.
- Astrobites habit worth keeping: be explicit about **what the authors actually did** (data, sample size, method) before what they found.

## Uncertainty and caveats

- Keep the authors' hedges ("suggests", "tentatively", "at 3σ"). Never upgrade a hint into a discovery.
- Give the strongest **alternative explanation** a fair hearing, ideally one already discussed in the paper or the literature it cites.
- State **what would settle it**: the next observation, instrument or dataset.
- Confident where the science is settled, careful where it isn't. Don't hedge textbook facts.

## Endings

- **Never summarise.** End on one of:
  - a return to the opening image or anecdote (bookending),
  - the open question, stated sharply,
  - the next step ("the next data release, due in 2027, should tell"),
  - a short, dry kicker.
- Last paragraph ≤ 3 sentences.

## Things to avoid (seen even in good outlets)

- Undefined terms left for a hyperlink to explain.
- Researchers' names misspelled: check every name against the paper's author list.
- Confident claims with no caveat on a single-field / single-object result.
- Captions that only give credit: our captions **tell the reader what to look at**.
- Press-release tone: "groundbreaking", "unprecedented", "sheds new light", "paves the way".

## AI tells (banned)

"delve", "tapestry", "realm", "testament to", "it's important/worth noting", "in conclusion", "navigating the complexities", "a symphony of", "unlock the secrets", "Moreover," at sentence start, triplets of adjectives, every paragraph ending in a neat moral, rhetorical-question openers in every section, bullet-point walls in the body.

## Self-check before opening the PR

Read the draft once more and answer honestly:
1. Would a curious non-specialist keep reading after the first two sentences?
2. Does every number have an anchor?
3. Is every jargon word defined where it first appears?
4. Did I state where the main analogy breaks?
5. Are the authors' hedges intact? Is there an alternative explanation and a "what would settle it"?
6. Does the ending avoid summarising?
7. Does it obey every rule in `style-lessons.md`?

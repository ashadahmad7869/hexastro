Stand on a beach at night and look out to sea. A small light glows on the horizon. It could be a candle in a rowing boat a few hundred metres out, or a lighthouse twenty kilometres away. From where you stand, the two look identical. Brightness alone can't tell you which one you're looking at.

Every point of light in the night sky has exactly this problem. A dim star can be a feeble one nearby or a brilliant one very far away. This guide is about how astronomers break that tie: first by measuring brightness carefully, then by measuring a handful of distances with pure geometry, and then by using those few to calibrate everything else. The result is a chain of methods called the **cosmic distance ladder**. It reaches billions of light-years, and its bottom rung is something you can do with your thumb.

## The thumb trick

Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Close your left eye, then open it and close your right. Your thumb jumps sideways against the wall behind it. Now bring your thumb halfway towards your face and do it again: the jump gets bigger.

That jump is **parallax**, the apparent shift of a nearby object against a distant background when you look from two different places. The closer the object, the bigger the shift. Your eyes are about 6.5 cm apart, and at arm's length your thumb jumps by about 6 degrees. Know the gap between your eyes, measure the angle, and you can work out how far away your thumb is without a tape measure.

Astronomers do the same thing with the Earth's orbit. In January we look at a nearby star from one side of the Sun; six months later we look from the other side, 300 million kilometres away. Against the far more distant background stars, the nearby star appears to shift back and forth.

Here's the catch: the shifts are tiny. Astronomers measure them in **arcseconds**, where one arcsecond is 1/3,600 of a degree, roughly the width of a coin seen from 5 km away. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, has a parallax of about 0.77 arcseconds. Seen with the full width of Earth's orbit, that's like blinking at a thumb held up about 9 km away. No wonder it took until 1838, when Friedrich Bessel measured the parallax of the star 61 Cygni (about 0.3 arcseconds), for anyone to manage it.

## The physics: two equations and a backwards scale

### Distance from an angle

The geometry gives astronomers their favourite unit. If a star has a parallax of exactly one arcsecond, its distance is one **parsec** (short for "parallax arcsecond"): about 3.26 light-years, or 31 trillion kilometres. And because the angle is so small, distance and parallax are simply inverses:

$$
d\,[\text{pc}] = \frac{1}{p\,[\text{arcsec}]}
$$

Half the parallax, twice the distance. Proxima's 0.77 arcseconds puts it at 1.3 parsecs, or 4.2 light-years. The baseline in the diagram, the average Earth–Sun distance, is the **astronomical unit** (AU), about 150 million km. Its value was pinned down precisely by bouncing radar off Venus in the early 1960s, and that measurement is the real bottom of the ladder: get the AU wrong and every parallax distance is wrong by the same factor.

### Brightness on a log scale

Now for brightness. Astronomers still use a system that goes back to the Greek astronomer Hipparchus, who sorted the stars into six classes. The brightest were "first magnitude", the faintest he could see "sixth magnitude". That's why the scale runs backwards: a *bigger* magnitude means a *fainter* star.

In 1856 Norman Pogson made it exact: five magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in brightness, so one magnitude is a factor of about 2.512. For two stars with measured fluxes $F_1$ and $F_2$ (the light energy arriving per second per square metre):

$$
m_1 - m_2 = -2.5 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{F_1}{F_2}\right)
$$

In words: take the brightness ratio, count its factors of ten, and each factor of ten is 2.5 magnitudes. The scale stretches enormously. The Sun is about magnitude −26.7, Sirius (the brightest star at night) −1.5, the faintest stars you can see from a dark site about +6, and the deepest Hubble images reach about +30. The Sun outshines Sirius in our sky by about 13 billion times, with a "b".

### Putting them together

The magnitude we see, the **apparent magnitude** $m$, mixes up how powerful a star is with how far away it is. To compare stars fairly, astronomers define the **absolute magnitude** $M$: how bright the star would look from a standard distance of 10 parsecs. Because light spreads out over a sphere, its flux falls as $1/d^2$ (the inverse-square law), and the two magnitudes are linked by the **distance modulus**:

$$
m - M = 5 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d}{10\ \text{pc}}\right)
$$

Read it as a translator. If you know any two of apparent magnitude, absolute magnitude and distance, you get the third. A star 10 pc away has $m = M$; every factor of 10 further away adds 5 magnitudes. Our Sun, moved to 10 pc, would be magnitude 4.8: a faint star you'd struggle to spot from a city.

That equation is the beach puzzle in maths. Apparent magnitude is what you measure. Distance and absolute magnitude are tangled together. Untangle one, and you have the other.

## Climbing the ladder

Parallax gives distance directly, so for nearby stars we can read off their absolute magnitudes. That's the key move. Once we know how bright some *kind* of star really is, we can spot the same kind far away, measure its apparent magnitude and solve for distance. An object whose true brightness we know is called a **standard candle**, and this is where the lighthouse comes back: if you know you're looking at a lighthouse, and you know how bright lighthouses are, the beach puzzle is solved.

**Rung 1: parallax.** ESA's Hipparcos satellite (launched 1989) measured parallaxes to about a thousandth of an arcsecond, good to 10% out to about 100 parsecs. Its successor Gaia has done far better: its best parallaxes are good to a few hundredths of a thousandth of an arcsecond, roughly twice the apparent width of a coin on the Moon. For bright stars, that means 10% distances out to a few thousand parsecs, across a good part of our galaxy, for more than a billion stars.

**Rung 2: Cepheids and other standard candles.** In 1912 Henrietta Swan Leavitt, working at Harvard, published the periods of 25 pulsating stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy near our own. All of them were at essentially the same distance, and she noticed that the ones that pulsed more slowly were brighter. These stars, called **Cepheid variables**, swell and shrink with periods of days to months, and the period tells you the luminosity. Polaris, the North Star, is one, pulsing every four days. Calibrate the relation with Cepheids that have parallaxes, and any Cepheid you can see in a distant galaxy becomes a lighthouse. Edwin Hubble used them in the 1920s to show that the Andromeda nebula is a separate galaxy, far outside the Milky Way.

A second, independent candle comes from old red giant stars. As they age they brighten, up to a point where helium suddenly ignites in their cores, and that happens at almost the same luminosity in every star. So the brightest red giants in an old population all top out at the same magnitude, a sharp edge called the **tip of the red giant branch** (TRGB).

**Rung 3: Type Ia supernovae.** Cepheids fade into the crowd beyond a few tens of millions of parsecs. Further out, astronomers use **Type Ia supernovae**, the explosions of white dwarf stars, which for a few weeks can rival a whole galaxy in brightness. They aren't perfectly identical, but their peak brightness can be corrected using how quickly they fade, which makes them "standardisable". Their calibration comes from galaxies close enough to also hold Cepheids or TRGB stars.

**Rung 4: the expansion of the universe.** The further away a galaxy is, the faster cosmic expansion carries it from us. Supernovae measure that rate, the **Hubble constant**, which then turns a galaxy's redshift into a distance.

Each rung inherits the errors of the ones below it. A mistake at the bottom ripples all the way to the top. This has happened before: in the 1950s Walter Baade realised that two different types of Cepheid had been confused, and the distances to other galaxies roughly doubled overnight.

## Where the analogy breaks

Lighthouses are built to a spec. Stars aren't. No candle in the sky is perfectly standard: a Cepheid's brightness also depends a little on its chemical make-up, and every class of candle has to be *calibrated* rather than assumed. Real lighthouses also don't sit behind fog banks. Interstellar dust dims and reddens starlight (the same **reddening** we met in [the blackbody guide](/posts/blackbody-radiation)), and towards the centre of our galaxy it dims visible light by about 30 magnitudes, a factor of a trillion. So the real distance modulus carries an extra term for dust, $m - M = 5\log_{10}(d/10\ \text{pc}) + A$, where $A$ is the **extinction** in magnitudes. Leave it out and every star seems further away than it really is.

## What we still don't know

Almost every open question here comes down to one: is the ladder sound?

**The Hubble tension.** Climbing the ladder with Cepheids and supernovae, the SH0ES team gets a Hubble constant of about 73 km/s per megaparsec. Working it out instead from the early universe, using the cosmic microwave background measured by the Planck satellite and the standard cosmological model, gives about 67. That gap of about 8% is far larger than the stated uncertainties of either side. Either a step in the ladder has a hidden error, or something is missing from our model of the universe.

**Which candle to trust.** Distances using the TRGB, led by Wendy Freedman's group, have tended to give values around 70, in between. The arguments are about how to find the tip and which galaxies to include, and JWST's sharper infrared images are now re-checking Cepheids and red giants in the same galaxies.

**Gaia's tiny offset.** Gaia's parallaxes have a small systematic offset of a few hundredths of a milliarcsecond, measured using quasars, which are so distant their true parallax is zero. For nearby stars it doesn't matter. For distant Cepheids, whose parallaxes are only a little larger than the offset, it matters a lot, and Gaia's next data release, covering more years of observations, should shrink it.

**Distances for faint things.** Brown dwarfs, objects too small to fuse hydrogen, are common near the Sun, but the coldest ones are so faint that only the very closest have measured parallaxes. We still don't have a full census of them.

## Key terms

- **Parallax:** the apparent shift of a nearby object against a distant background, seen from two places; for stars, measured from opposite sides of Earth's orbit.
- **Parsec (pc):** the distance at which a star's parallax is one arcsecond; about 3.26 light-years.
- **Apparent magnitude ($m$):** how bright an object looks from Earth, on a backwards log scale where 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100.
- **Absolute magnitude ($M$):** how bright an object would look from 10 parsecs away.
- **Distance modulus:** $m - M$, which depends only on distance (plus dust).
- **Standard candle:** an object whose true brightness is known, so its apparent brightness gives its distance.
- **Cepheid variable:** a pulsating star whose period reveals its luminosity.
- **Extinction:** dimming of starlight by interstellar dust, in magnitudes.

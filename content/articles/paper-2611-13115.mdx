In the halo of our own Galaxy, the most iron-poor stars are usually rich in carbon. Among those with less than a ten-thousandth of the Sun's iron, more than 80% are. Next door, in the small galaxies that orbit the Milky Way, the pattern mostly vanishes. In the Sculptor dwarf galaxy, astronomers have searched for years and come up nearly empty.

Now a team led by Francisco Cuadra at the University of Florence has taken a long, sharp look at one of the exceptions: a faint red giant called DR20080. It is carbon-rich, extraordinarily iron-poor, and, as it turns out, not quite like any star measured before. The paper, accepted in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, argues that its chemistry is the leftover ash of two of the very first stars in the universe.

## The backstory: reading ash

The first stars formed from the raw material of the Big Bang: hydrogen and helium, nothing else. Astronomers call them Population III. They were probably massive and short-lived, and none has ever been seen. But when they exploded they seeded their surroundings with the first carbon, oxygen, iron and everything else, and the next generation of stars formed from that seasoned gas.

Some of those second-generation stars were small. A star of less than about 0.8 solar masses burns so slowly that it outlives the current age of the universe, so some are still shining. Their outer layers preserve the gas they were born from, a little like ash in a cold fireplace: look carefully and you can tell what kind of wood burned there.

You read that ash with exactly the tool from [our guide to light and spectra](/posts/light-and-spectra). Every element absorbs light at its own set of wavelengths, so a star's spectrum is a barcode of what's in its atmosphere. The stars this paper is about are called **CEMP-no**: *carbon-enhanced, metal-poor*, with *no* boost in heavy elements like barium. They are widely thought to be direct descendants of the first stars. The carbon is the giveaway. Theorists expect some first stars to have died as *faint supernovae*, explosions too weak to fling out the iron from deep inside but still able to throw off the lighter carbon from the outer layers.

The puzzle is where these stars live. They're common in the Milky Way's halo and in the tiniest "ultra-faint" dwarf galaxies, but strikingly rare in bigger dwarfs like Sculptor. Before this paper, only one Sculptor star had been confirmed as CEMP-no with high-resolution spectra, and it was neither especially iron-poor nor especially carbon-rich. If the first stars behaved differently depending on where they formed, stars like DR20080 are where you'd see it.

## What they did

Sculptor sits about 280,000 light-years away (86 kiloparsecs), so even its brightest giants are faint. The team pointed the UVES spectrograph on ESO's Very Large Telescope at DR20080 eight times between October and November 2022, for 50 minutes each, about 6.7 hours in total. They then stacked the exposures.

UVES spreads light out finely enough to separate two features whose wavelengths differ by just 1 part in 35,000 (its *resolving power*). That is enough to pick out individual absorption lines from 17 elements, and to set upper limits on 4 more whose lines were too weak to see. Each line's depth was turned into an abundance using model stellar atmospheres. The team then corrected for known biases: the simple models assume the gas is in perfect thermal balance, which isn't quite true in thin giant-star atmospheres.

## What they found

**It is very iron-poor.** DR20080 has an iron abundance of [Fe/H] = −3.26. The scale is logarithmic, so that means roughly 1/1,800th of the Sun's iron for every hydrogen atom.

**It is very carbon-rich.** Relative to its iron, it has about 40 times more carbon than the Sun ([C/Fe] = +1.60). That figure includes a +0.55 correction: as a giant ages, it stirs some of its surface carbon down into the interior to be burned, so the team adds back what was lost.

**It is barium-poor,** at [Ba/Fe] = −1.28, about a twentieth of the solar ratio. That rules out the usual alternative route to a carbon-rich star, a companion star dumping carbon *and* barium onto it (more on that below).

**And then the oddities.** Oxygen is strongly enhanced ([O/Fe] = +1.53), higher than the average for comparable Milky Way stars. Manganese and nickel are well below solar ([Mn/Fe] = −1.02, [Ni/Fe] = −0.45). In stars this iron-poor, nickel usually tracks iron almost exactly, so a deficit is unusual. Silicon is low too, and the authors note that sub-solar silicon is extremely rare in CEMP-no stars.

Put together, the pattern doesn't match the average of 39 comparable carbon-rich Milky Way stars or of 8 in ultra-faint dwarfs. The authors call it unique, at least among the stars measured so far.

**Who were the ancestors?** The team compared the pattern with a grid of predicted supernova yields, varying the dying star's mass and explosion energy, and asked which combinations fit best. One progenitor fits poorly. Two fit much better. In the best fit, about 95% of the star's ingredients come from a 43-solar-mass first-generation star that died as a *faint* supernova, at roughly 0.6 times the energy of a typical supernova. The remaining 5% comes from a 19.4-solar-mass *hypernova*, about ten times more energetic than a typical supernova. When the fit is restricted to the 10 elements that models reproduce best, the main ancestor comes out the same; only the minor one shifts, to a 50-solar-mass hypernova contributing 15%.

There's a neat twist. The minor ancestor looks like the one previously inferred for AS0039, the most iron-poor star known in Sculptor: a hypernova of about 20 solar masses. The authors raise the possibility that the *same* explosion enriched both stars, though they flag that this should be treated with caution.

## Why it matters

The first stars can't be observed directly, so everything we know about their masses and deaths comes from stars like this one. Each well-measured descendant is another data point on how massive they were and how they exploded.

DR20080 also feeds the "where" question. The authors read its uniqueness as support for the idea that carbon-rich descendants form differently depending on the galaxy they're born in. That would mean the first stars, or the gas around them, varied from place to place.

## The fine print

A sceptic's first question would be: is the carbon really ancient? A carbon-rich star can also get its carbon later, from a companion star that swells up and spills material onto it. But that route brings barium with it, and DR20080 is barium-poor. Its velocity also hasn't changed between 2018 and 2022, so there's no sign of a companion, although the authors note a binary can't be fully excluded.

Other caveats, all from the paper:

- **One star.** "Unique" is a strong word for a single object. The comparison samples are a few dozen stars.
- **Some elements rest on one line.** Oxygen comes from a single weak line, so the team deliberately widened its error bar. Nickel is also based on one line, though weaker lines elsewhere agree.
- **The models aren't perfect.** Titanium and cobalt are still poorly reproduced, a known problem with supernova yield models even for later stars. Restricting the fit to the reliable elements improves the match but makes the answer less certain, because fewer constraints allow more combinations to fit.
- **A different ancestor is possible.** The carbon-to-oxygen ratio is also consistent with *spinstars*, fast-spinning massive first stars, and the authors say this origin can't be ruled out.
- **It might be an immigrant.** An earlier study found that DR20080 belongs to a group of very iron-poor Sculptor stars that may have been captured when Sculptor swallowed an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy. If so, it says less about Sculptor itself, and the rarity of these stars in dwarfs like Sculptor becomes even more striking.

What would settle it is more stars like this. Each additional iron-poor, carbon-rich star in a galaxy like Sculptor, measured at this level of detail, tests whether DR20080 is a one-off or the first member of a family.

## Jargon box

- **Population III**: the first generation of stars, made only of hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang.
- **[X/Fe]**: the logarithmic ratio of element X to iron, compared with the Sun. +1 means ten times the solar ratio; −1 means a tenth.
- **CEMP-no star**: carbon-enhanced ([C/Fe] above +0.7), metal-poor, with no excess of heavy elements like barium.
- **Faint supernova**: a low-energy explosion in which much of the inner iron falls back onto the remnant, so mainly lighter elements escape.
- **Hypernova**: an unusually energetic supernova, here about ten times the energy of a typical one.
- **Dwarf spheroidal galaxy (dSph)**: a small, old, dark-matter-dominated satellite galaxy such as Sculptor. *Ultra-faint dwarfs* are smaller and dimmer still.
- **Spinstar**: a massive, rapidly rotating early star whose mixing changes the elements it releases.
